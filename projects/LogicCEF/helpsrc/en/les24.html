A burnt contact

Goal: see that a poor or burnt contact takes voltage away from the loads, measure that voltage drop with a voltmeter, and understand why such a contact gets warm.

▶ Open this example

The circuit

  • The source is set to 12 V, without internal resistance (Ri = 0 Ω).
  • The + terminal goes through a 500 mA fuse, the milliammeter and a contact to the + rail. The contact starts as a poor contact.
  • The top voltmeter sits across the contact: + on the terminal on the source side, − on the terminal on the rail side. It measures the difference: the voltage drop.
  • The bottom voltmeter measures the voltage on the + rail: what is left for the lamps.

Voltage drop and heat

The current of all the lamps flows through the contact. There is then a voltage across the contact:

U = I × R

That voltage is lost: the lamps get 12 V minus the voltage drop. So the two voltmeters together always show 12 V. With three lamps on you measure:

ContactMilliammeterAcross the contactOn the + rail
good contact (0.05 Ω)303 mA0.0 V12.0 V
long thin wire (1 Ω)296 mA0.3 V11.7 V
poor contact (2 Ω)288 mA0.6 V11.4 V
burnt contact (10 Ω)242 mA2.4 V9.6 V

The contact also gets warm. The power lost in it is P = U × I = I × I × R:

  • good contact: 0.303 A × 0.303 A × 0.05 Ω = 0.005 W: you notice nothing;
  • burnt contact: 0.242 A × 0.242 A × 10 Ω = 0.59 W: more than a hundred times as much, in one small spot. The block turns red.

In real life that is dangerous: a warm contact oxidises, which makes the resistance even larger, and it gets even warmer. That is how a loose terminal in a socket or a cable lug can start a fire.

All formulas (U, I, R and P) with the triangle are in The formulas.

Try it

  1. Start the simulation and turn on the three switches. Write down the current and the two voltages.
  2. Right-click the contact and choose Kind › good contact, then long thin wire and burnt contact. Write down the meters each time and look at the colour of the block.
  3. Each time, add up the two voltages.

Exercise

  1. Leave the contact on burnt contact and turn two lamps off. What do the meters show now? Why is the voltage drop smaller?
  2. Why does the voltmeter across a good contact show 0.0 V, even though 303 mA flows through it?
  3. A lamp is about 120 Ω (see lesson 23). Calculate the current and the two voltages with two lamps and a burnt contact. Then check with the meters.
Solution
  1. 95 mA, 0.9 V across the contact and 11.1 V on the rail. Less current gives less voltage drop (U = I × R). That is why an electrician always measures a suspect contact under load: without current you see nothing.
  2. U = 0.303 A × 0.05 Ω = 0.015 V. That is too small for the meter, which shows one decimal.
  3. Two lamps in parallel: 120 Ω / 2 = 60 Ω. With the contact: 70 Ω. I = 12 V / 70 Ω = 0.171 A. Across the contact: 0.171 A × 10 Ω = 1.7 V, on the rail 12 V − 1.7 V = 10.3 V. The meter shows 174 mA: the switches draw a tiny bit more.
