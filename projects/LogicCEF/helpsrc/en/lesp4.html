The distribution line: rails

Goal: connect many loads to one source with a + rail (supply) and a − rail (return), and see that the currents add up.

Water in a house

In a house, not every tap has its own pipe to the water main in the street. There is one main pipe that brings in the water, and every tap takes a branch from it. The waste water leaves through one drain.

In lesson 3 every circuit went back to the source with its own wires. With ten lamps that becomes a tangle of wires. That is why from now on we use rails:

  • the red + rail is the supply line: it gets the voltage of the + terminal of the source;
  • the blue − rail is the return, back to the − terminal of the source (0 V, ground);
  • every load sits between the two rails: supply on the left, return on the right.

That way every circuit looks the same: the energy comes in on the left and goes back on the right.

Try it

  1. Start the simulation and close one switch. Read the milliammeter.
  2. Close a second and a third switch. What does the meter do? And the lamps that were already on?
  3. Look at the electrons in the rails: where do they go fastest?

Exercise

How much current does the meter measure with 1, 2 and 3 lamps? Why does a lamp not get dimmer when you add one? What does that mean for the thickness of the main pipe in a house?

Solution

100, 200 and 300 mA: the currents of the lamps add up in the supply. Each lamp sits directly between the rails and so gets the full 12 V; that is why it stays just as bright. The lamps are in parallel. The main line must be able to carry the current of all loads together: it is thicker than the wire to one lamp. That is also why there is a fuse in it.

Good to know: in the lessons with gates (such as lesson 10) the rails are also the power supply of push buttons and lamps: you then plug those straight onto the rail. See Plugging in.
