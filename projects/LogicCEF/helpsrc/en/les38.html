The transistor as a switch

Goal: see how a small current into the base of a transistor switches a much larger current through a lamp on and off.

▶ Open this example

Two circuits

A transistor has three terminals: the base B, the collector C and the emitter E. The small current through the base (Ib) decides how much current may flow from the collector to the emitter (Ic).

  1. Start the simulation with the switch open. No base current flows: the transistor is off (grey), the lamp is off and the voltmeter shows 12.0 V between C and E. The transistor is an open switch.
  2. Close the switch. The bottom meter shows a base current of 1.1 mA: Ib = (12 − 0.7) V / 10 kΩ. There are always about 0.7 V between B and E, as with a diode.
  3. The lamp lights, and the top meter shows 98 mA. A current of 1.1 mA switches on a current that is almost 90 times larger.
  4. The transistor is saturated (green) and the voltmeter shows only 0.2 V: the transistor is fully on, like a closed switch. Almost all the voltage is across the lamp.

Why saturated?

With β = 100 the transistor would let 100 × 1.1 mA = 113 mA through. But the 120 Ω lamp lets at most (12 − 0.2) V / 120 Ω ≈ 98 mA through. So the transistor is wide open and the lamp limits the current. This is how you use a transistor as a switch: the base current is more than large enough.

Task: a base resistor that is too large

Right-click the resistor and choose Kind › series resistor (100 kΩ). What does the lamp do now, and what do the meters show?

Solution

The base current becomes ten times smaller: 0.11 mA. The transistor now lets 100 × 0.11 mA = 11 mA through and is amplifying (orange). That is too little for the lamp: it hardly glows, and there are 10.6 V between C and E. As a switch the transistor only works well when the base current is large enough. How it amplifies is shown in the next lesson.

Note: never connect the base to + without a resistor. Between B and E there is a diode: without a resistor so much base current flows that the transistor breaks. Feel free to try it with the resistor set to 1 Ω; right-click › Replace transistor repairs it.
In real life: an output of a microcontroller (such as an Arduino) only supplies a few mA. With a transistor it can still drive a relay, a motor or a bright lamp.
