Power

Goal: work out the power P = U × I, see why a strong lamp in series with a weak lamp stays dark, and choose a resistor that does not get too hot.

▶ Open this example

What is power?

Power says how much energy per second a device turns into light or heat. You measure it in watts (W):

P = U × I    and with Ohm's law also    P = U2 / R

A 24 Ω lamp at 12 V: P = 12 × 12 / 24 = 6 W. A 480 Ω lamp: P = 144 / 480 = 0.3 W. The smaller the resistance, the bigger the power at the same voltage.

  1. Switch on rows 2 and 3. Both lamps are fully lit. The milliammeters show 500 mA and 25 mA: P = 12 V × 0.5 A = 6 W and 12 V × 0.025 A = 0.3 W.
  2. Look at the wattmeters: 6.00 W and 0.30 W. They work out U × I for you.

A strong and a weak lamp in series

  1. Switch rows 2 and 3 off and row 1 on. Which lamp lights?
  2. Surprise: the weak 0.3 W lamp is almost fully lit, the strong 6 W lamp stays dark. The milliammeter shows only 24 mA.

In series the same current flows through both lamps: I = 12 V / (24 + 480) Ω ≈ 24 mA. As with the voltage divider, the biggest resistance gets the biggest part of the voltage: 11.4 V across the 480 Ω lamp, but only 0.6 V across the 24 Ω lamp. The power:

P = U × I = 11.4 V × 0.024 A ≈ 0.27 W    and    0.6 V × 0.024 A ≈ 0.014 W

  1. The wattmeter of row 1 shows 0.29 W: the two lamps together (0.27 + 0.014).
  2. Put the wattmeter between the two lamps: undo the wires and connect it between the 24 Ω lamp and the 480 Ω lamp. Now it shows 0.27 W: it only measures what comes after it, the 480 Ω lamp.

A resistor gets warm

  1. Switch on row 4. The 100 Ω resistor turns orange: it gets hot.
  2. The milliammeter shows 120 mA, the wattmeter 1.44 W: P = 12 V × 0.12 A = 1.44 W. All the energy becomes heat.

Assignment: a quarter-watt resistor

Ordinary small resistors can only take 0.25 W; above that they burn. Which resistor can you safely put in row 4 at 12 V? Choose 100, 220, 470 or 1000 Ω and check with the wattmeter.

Solution

Needed: P = 144 / R ≤ 0.25, so R ≥ 576 Ω. Only 1000 Ω is safe: P = 144 / 1000 = 0.144 W. With 470 Ω it is already 0.31 W, just too much.

In real life: every appliance shows its power: an 8 W LED bulb, a 2000 W kettle. The electricity meter counts power times time, in kilowatt-hours (kWh).
