Half-wave rectification

Goal: use one diode to turn an AC voltage into a voltage that always has the same direction, and see the difference on two oscilloscopes.

▶ Open this example

The diode lets half through

  1. Start the simulation. The left oscilloscope shows the AC voltage of the source: a sine from +12 to −12 V.
  2. The right oscilloscope shows the voltage across the lamp: only the positive half waves. In between it is 0 V.
  3. Look at the diode: in the positive half it conducts (salmon red), in the negative half it blocks (grey).
  4. The lamp now gets bright only once per period, and then stays dark for half a period.

The voltage across the lamp no longer changes direction: it is rectified. Because only one half of each wave is used, this is called half-wave rectification. The peak across the lamp is 12 − 0.7 = 11.3 V: the diode takes 0.7 V.

Rails after the diode

After the diode the voltage no longer changes direction. That is why the diode feeds a + rail; the other terminal of the source goes to the − rail. The lamp and the right oscilloscope sit between the rails, as in the previous lessons. Watch the number above the + rail: it rises to 11.3 V and drops back to 0 V, but never goes negative.

Assignment: the other half

Turn the diode round (right-click › Turn round). What does the right oscilloscope show now?

Solution

Now only the negative half waves get through: the voltage across the lamp is always negative, down to −11.3 V. It is still rectified, but in the other direction.

In real life: half-wave rectification throws away half of the energy and gives a voltage with big gaps. That is why most power supplies use four diodes: the Graetz bridge of the next lesson.
