The transformer

Goal: see how a transformer with two coils on one iron core steps an alternating voltage up or down, learn the rule U2 / U1 = N2 / N1, and understand why it only works with alternating voltage.

Two coils, one core

A transformer has a closed iron core with two coils on it:

  • the primary coil (N1 turns) is connected to an alternating voltage. It makes a magnetic field in the core (lesson 2), and because the current keeps changing direction, that field changes with it;
  • the core leads that changing field through the secondary coil (N2 turns). There the field keeps changing, and that induces a voltage: induction, as in lesson 7.

Every turn gets the same voltage. If the secondary coil has half as many turns, it gets half the voltage; twice as many turns gives twice the voltage:

Transformer rule: U2 / U1 = N2 / N1

Try it

  1. Start the simulation. The alternating voltage source gives 12 V to the primary coil. Below N1 and N2 you see the voltage of each coil. It goes up and down with the alternating voltage; the highest values are about 12 V and 6 V. The lamp glows dimly.
  2. Right-click the transformer and choose 200 under Secondary turns: now U2 equals U1, and the lamp glows fully.
  3. Choose 50 secondary turns: U2 becomes a quarter, about 3 V.
  4. Set the secondary back to 100 and choose 100 under Primary turns: now N2 equals N1, and U2 is 12 V again.
  5. Point at the transformer: the hint also shows the currents I1 and I2.

Voltage down, current up

A transformer does not make energy. What it gains in voltage it loses in current, and the other way round: U1 × I1 is about U2 × I2. If it halves the voltage, the secondary can give twice as much current as the primary takes.

Only alternating voltage

Replace the alternating voltage source with an ordinary source (a battery, direct voltage). After switching on, the field in the core stays the same, and a field that does not change induces no voltage. The secondary then gives nothing, and the primary coil is just a wire with resistance: a lot of current flows and it gets warm. That is why the electricity grid uses alternating voltage.

Where is there a transformer?

  • In a charger or adapter: from 230 V to, for example, 5 V.
  • In the high-voltage grid: next to the power station a transformer steps the voltage up to hundreds of thousands of volts. The same energy then goes through the long lines with much less current, and they get less warm. Near the houses transformers step the voltage down again to 230 V.

Exercise

1. The primary coil has 200 turns and gets 12 V. How many turns must the secondary coil have for 6 V? And for 24 V? Check it in the example.

2. Why is electricity sent over long distances at a very high voltage?

Solution

1. For 6 V: N2 = 200 × 6 / 12 = 100 turns. For 24 V: N2 = 200 × 24 / 12 = 400 turns. (In Logic U2 is a little lower, because of a small voltage loss in the secondary coil.)

2. The power is P = U × I. At a high voltage only a small current is needed for the same power. A small current loses much less energy as heat in the long wires.
