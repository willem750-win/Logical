Magnetism in a coil

Goal: see that an electric current makes a magnetic field, that a coil (solenoid) with a current behaves like a bar magnet, and that the direction of the current decides where the north pole is.

A current makes a magnet

In 1820 the Danish physicist Hans Christian Ørsted saw something strange: a compass needle next to a wire turned away as soon as a current flowed through the wire. Without a current it simply pointed north. So an electric current makes a magnetic field: around a straight wire the field lines lie in circles round the wire.

One wire gives only a weak field. Wind the wire into a coil with many turns and the fields of all the turns add up. Such a long coil is also called a solenoid.

  • Inside the coil the field is strong and runs straight through, from the south pole to the north pole.
  • Outside the coil the field looks just like that of a bar magnet: the field lines run from N to S.
  • So the field lines are closed loops: outside from N to S, inside from S back to N.

Try it

  1. Start the simulation. The switch is open: no current flows, the coil is not a magnet and both compasses point north.
  2. Close the switch. The lamp lights, current flows through the coil, and the field lines appear at once. Below the coil there is now an N and an S, and the compasses turn.
  3. Point at the coil with the mouse: the hint says where the north pole is and how strong the coil is.
  4. Open the switch again: the field disappears. That is the big advantage of an electromagnet: you can switch it on and off.
  5. Right-click the coil and choose Wound the other way. N and S change places, the arrows on the field lines reverse and the compasses turn half a turn.

Where is the north pole? The right-hand rule

Which end becomes the north pole depends on the direction in which the current goes round the coil. There is a handy way to remember it:

Right-hand rule: put your right hand round the coil so that your fingers curl along with the current through the turns. Your thumb then points to the north pole.

So there are two ways to swap N and S:

  • reverse the current: in a real experiment you swap the two wires at the battery;
  • wind the coil the other way: then the same current goes round the other way. In Logic you do that with Wound the other way.

Turning a coil end over end does not help: the winding turns with it, and the north pole stays on the same side of the current.

Exercise

Right-click the coil and choose Rotate, so that it stands upright. Close the switch. Where is the north pole now? Put a compass below the coil and one above it. Where do they point?

Solution

Upright, the left terminal comes to the top, so the current now enters at the top. The north pole is then at the bottom (the hint says so too). The compass below the coil points down, away from the north pole; the compass above the coil also points down, towards the south pole. If you then choose Wound the other way, everything reverses.
