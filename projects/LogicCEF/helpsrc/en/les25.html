Lamps in parallel

Goal: see that lamps in parallel each get the full voltage, that their currents add up, and calculate the total resistance. You compare with lamps in series.

▶ Open this example

The circuit

  • The source is set to 12 V, without internal resistance (Ri = 0 Ω).
  • The + terminal goes through a fuse of 500 mA and the milliammeter to the left wire.
  • Between the left and the right wire there are three branches, each with a switch and a lamp of 120 Ω. The right wire goes back to the − terminal of the source.
  • The voltmeter is across the bottom lamp (lamp C).
  • Only the switch of lamp A is closed.

Parallel: every lamp gets 12 V

Every branch is connected directly between the + and the − of the source. So every lamp gets the full voltage of 12 V and shines just as brightly, however many lamps are on. Through every lamp flows I = U / R = 12 V / 120 Ω = 100 mA. The milliammeter measures the current of all branches together:

I = IA + IB + IC

The more lamps in parallel, the smaller the total resistance: the current gets more paths. You calculate the total resistance like this:

1 / R = 1 / RA + 1 / RB + 1 / RC

With equal lamps it is simple: divide the resistance of one lamp by the number of lamps. Three lamps of 120 Ω: 120 Ω / 3 = 40 Ω, and 12 V / 40 Ω = 300 mA.

Compare: lamps in series

If you put two lamps one after the other in one wire, they are in series. The same current flows through both, and the resistances add up: R = R1 + R2 = 240 Ω. The current is then 12 V / 240 Ω = 50 mA, and every lamp only gets half of the voltage: 6 V.

Try it

  1. Start the simulation. Lamp A is on, the meter shows 100 mA. The voltmeter across lamp C shows 0.0 V: without current through the lamp there is no voltage across it (U = I × R = 0).
  2. Click the lever of the switches of B and C. Write down the current each time. Lamp A does not get dimmer.
  3. Hold the mouse over a lamp: the hint shows U, I and P of that lamp.

Assignment

  1. Switch on all three lamps. Right-click lamp C and choose Resistance › 60 Ω. First calculate the current through lamp C and the total current, then check with the meter.
  2. Now choose 24 Ω for lamp C. What happens? Why?
  3. Build two lamps of 120 Ω in series on 12 V yourself, with a voltmeter across one lamp. What current do you measure, and what voltage? Does a lamp shine more or less brightly than here?
  4. Why are the lamps and sockets in a house connected in parallel and not in series?
Tip: want two wires on the same terminal? Click the Connection button and then the terminal, even if a wire is already on it: a new wire starts.
Solution
  1. Lamp C: 12 V / 60 Ω = 200 mA. Total: 100 + 100 + 200 = 400 mA. With the formula: 1 / R = 1/120 + 1/120 + 1/60 = 4/120, so R = 30 Ω and 12 V / 30 Ω = 400 mA. Lamp C is a 2.4 W lamp.
  2. Lamp C needs 12 V / 24 Ω = 500 mA. Together with A and B that is 700 mA, more than the 500 mA of the fuse: it blows and all lamps go out. Switch one off, choose a smaller lamp and replace the fuse (right-click › Replace fuse).
  3. 12 V / 240 Ω = 50 mA and 6.0 V across every lamp. A lamp then gets 6 V × 0.05 A = 0.3 W instead of 1.2 W: it shines much less brightly.
  4. In parallel every appliance gets the full voltage, you can switch every appliance on and off separately, and if one lamp breaks, the rest keeps working. The current of all appliances together does go through the fuse of that circuit.
