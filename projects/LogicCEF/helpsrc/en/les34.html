The diode

Goal: see that a diode lets current through in one direction only, and measure the voltage across the diode in each direction.

▶ Open this example

Forward direction

A diode has an anode A and a cathode K (at the bar). Current only flows from A to K, and only above 0.7 V.

  1. Switch on the top switch. The lamp lights and the diode turns salmon red: it conducts.
  2. The voltmeter across the diode shows 0.7 V. The rest, 11.3 V, is across the lamp.
  3. Point at the lamp: I = (12 − 0.7) V / 120 Ω ≈ 94 mA flows through it.

Reverse direction

  1. Switch on the bottom switch. The lamp stays off and the diode is grey: it blocks.
  2. The voltmeter across the diode shows 12.0 V: the full voltage is across the diode, and no current flows.
  3. Right-click the bottom diode and choose Turn round. Now the bottom lamp lights too.

So a diode is a kind of one-way street for the current.

Assignment: protection against wrong connection

A battery device can break if you put the battery in the wrong way round. Where do you put one diode so that no current can flow with a wrongly placed battery? Try it with the top branch: swap the two wires between the source and the rails.

Solution

In series with the device, with the anode to the + of the battery. With the battery the right way round, the diode conducts (0.7 V is lost). The wrong way round, the diode blocks and the device stays safe.

In real life: diodes are in almost every charger and power supply. An LED is a diode too, one that gives light.
