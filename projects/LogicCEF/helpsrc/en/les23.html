Ohm’s law

Goal: see that the current grows when more loads are on, that the voltage of the source then drops a little, and what a fuse does when the current becomes too large.

▶ Open this example

The circuit

  • The source is set to 12 V and has an internal resistance Ri = 2 Ω.
  • The + terminal goes through a 250 mA fuse and a milliammeter to the + rail.
  • Between the + rail and the − rail there are three branches, each with a switch and a lamp of 120 Ω.
  • The voltmeter measures the voltage of the + rail.

Current, voltage and resistance

At 12 V a lamp draws a current of 100 mA. According to Ohm’s law its resistance is:

R = U / I = 12 V / 0.1 A = 120 Ω

Lamps side by side (in parallel) on the rails each get the same voltage. Their currents add up: two lamps together draw twice as much current. The source has to supply that current, and because of its internal resistance the voltage at its terminals drops a little: U = E − I × Ri.

With two lamps: 12 V − 0.194 A × 2 Ω = 11.6 V. With three lamps the current would be 12 V / (40 Ω + 2 Ω) = 286 mA. That is more than the 250 mA of the fuse: it blows and the circuit is broken.

All formulas (U, I, R and P) with the triangle are in The formulas.

Look inside the wire

What actually happens inside the wire? Move the voltage from 0 to 12 V. At 0 V the electrons stand still. The higher the voltage, the faster they move: the current gets larger and the lamp shines brighter. At 12 V you see the same 100 mA as in the table above. More explanation in Look inside the wire: the electrons flow.

In the example of this lesson you also see the electrons in the wires: the source is set to Show electrons. Switch on more lamps and they move faster. See The source.

Try it

  1. Start the simulation. Switch the lamps on one by one and write down the current and the voltage each time.
  2. Switch on the third lamp: the fuse blows, all lamps go out and the + rail turns grey.
  3. Switch one lamp off. Right-click the fuse and choose Replace fuse: the two lamps are on again.

Exercise

  1. Set the source to 6 V with the slider and let one lamp shine. First work out with R = 120 Ω which current you expect, then check with the meter.
  2. Set the source back to 12 V. Right-click the source and choose Internal resistance › 0 Ω. What does the voltmeter do now when you switch lamps on and off?
  3. Which fuse do you need so that the three lamps can shine together?
Solution
  1. I = U / R = 6 V / 120 Ω = 0.05 A = 50 mA. The meter shows 49 mA: because of the internal resistance of 2 Ω slightly less current flows.
  2. The voltmeter stays at 12.0 V. Without internal resistance the terminal voltage does not drop, however much current you draw. Such an ideal source does not exist in real life.
  3. Three lamps together need about 286 mA. The 250 mA fuse is too small; choose (right-click › Rated current) 500 mA. You always pick a fuse a little larger than the normal current, but not much larger, or it no longer protects anything.
