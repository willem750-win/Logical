The dynamo

Goal: see that a rotating magnet next to a coil produces an alternating voltage, and know what the speed does to the voltage and the frequency. That is how a bicycle dynamo and the generator in a power station work.

From pulse to alternating voltage

In lesson 7 the coil gave a short pulse of current each time: one way when the magnet went in, the other way when it came out. Let the magnet spin next to the coil and first the north pole points at the coil, then the south pole, then the north pole again, and so on. So the field through the coil keeps changing direction, and the coil gives a current that keeps reversing: an alternating current.

That is how a bicycle dynamo works: the little wheel spins a magnet inside a coil. In a power station steam, wind or flowing water turns a turbine, which spins a large magnet in a generator.

The example

On the left is a rotating magnet: a strong bar magnet on an axle. Next to it lies a coil with 400 turns and an iron core. The coil is connected in series with a lamp and a centre-zero meter. There is no source: all the energy comes from the rotating magnet.

Try it

  1. Start the simulation and click the rotating magnet: it turns at 120 rpm, that is twice per second. The needle of the meter swings left and right each time: that is alternating current.
  2. Look at the lamp: it lights up twice per turn. It glows at every peak of the current, whichever way it flows.
  3. Right-click the magnet and choose 30 rpm under Speed: the needle moves slowly and swings only a little, and the lamp stays nearly dark.
  4. Choose 240 rpm: the needle swings back and forth quickly and far, and the lamp glows much brighter.
  5. Click the magnet again: it stops, and everything is off.

Alternating voltage

An alternating voltage keeps changing direction. How often that happens per second is the frequency, in hertz (Hz). For the dynamo the frequency equals the number of turns per second: 120 rpm is 2 Hz. The socket at home gives an alternating voltage of 50 Hz; for that the generator in the power station turns 3000 times a minute. You can find more about alternating voltage in lesson 35.

Remember: when the dynamo turns faster, the voltage gets higher and the frequency larger, both at once.

Why cycling gets harder

According to Lenz’s law the induced current opposes the motion: the coil brakes the rotating magnet. The more current the lamp takes, the harder that brake. That is why cycling is harder with the dynamo on, and why a power station burns more fuel when more electricity is used. The energy in the lamp comes from the motion.

Exercise

1. Drag an oscilloscope from the component toolbar and connect its two terminals to the two terminals of the lamp. Let the magnet turn at 120 rpm and set the scope to 4 s. How many waves do you see on the screen? What is the frequency?

2. Why does the lamp glow brighter when the magnet turns faster?

Solution

1. About 8 waves in 4 seconds: 2 per second, so 2 Hz. That is exactly 120 turns per minute divided by 60.

2. The faster the magnet turns, the faster the field through the coil changes. A faster change gives a higher induced voltage (lesson 7), so a larger current through the lamp.
