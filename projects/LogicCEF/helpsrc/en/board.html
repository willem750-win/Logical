A real Arduino board

Logic can drive a real Arduino Uno (or Nano) through the USB cable. A real push button on the board then operates a circuit in Logic, and Logic lights a real LED on the board. You build a circuit on the screen first and then see it really work.

What do you need?

  • An Arduino Uno or Nano (or a clone) with a USB cable, a breadboard, a few LEDs, 220 Ω resistors and push buttons. A starter kit has all of it.
  • The free Arduino IDE, to put the program StandardFirmata on the board once: File › Examples › Firmata › StandardFirmata, then Upload. Logic talks to the board in the Firmata language; after that you no longer need the Arduino IDE.
  • Cheap clones often use a CH340 chip for USB. Windows usually installs the driver itself; otherwise you find it with the Arduino IDE.

The two components

ButtonNameWhat is it?
Arduino inputA push button with two terminals in the circuit, like the ordinary push button. It is closed while the real push button on the board is pressed. Connect the button between the pin (D2 by default) and GND; no resistor is needed, Logic turns on the internal pull-up of the pin. Without a board you press it with the mouse.
Arduino outputA 5 V lamp with two terminals. When it lights (e.g. on the output of a gate), Logic sets the real pin high (5 V), otherwise low. Connect an LED with a 220 Ω resistor to GND on the pin (D13 by default). D13 also has a small LED on the board itself.

With right-click › Arduino pin you choose another pin: D2 to D13 (and for an input also A0 to A5). Both components count in the truth table.

Are the two buttons missing? They are in the component toolbar from the level Advanced: choose Panel choice › Level.

Connecting

  1. Plug the board into a USB port.
  2. In the top bar, click Arduino board (left of the magnifier).
  3. Choose the port (e.g. COM3, on Linux /dev/ttyACM0) and click Connect. The board restarts briefly; after two seconds it says Connected ... (Firmata 2.5).
  4. Start the simulation and press the real button.

Note: a pin gives at most about 20 mA. An LED with a resistor is fine; drive a lamp, motor or relay through a transistor or a relay module (see lesson 42). Never connect 230 V to the board.

A real servo

A servo motor on the panel can make a real servo turn along. Right-click the servo, choose Real servo on the Arduino board and a pin (e.g. D9). Connect the servo like this: brown or black to GND, red to 5V, orange or yellow to the chosen pin. Logic puts that pin in servo mode and sends the angle of the arm on the screen. See lesson 44.

A small servo (such as the SG90 from a starter kit) may use the 5V of the board. A bigger servo gets its own supply; then connect the GND of that supply to the GND of the board.

Not working?

  • No port found: plug the board in again and click Find ports. In Windows, look in Device Manager › Ports (COM & LPT).
  • Cannot open the port: close the Arduino IDE (or the serial monitor); only one program at a time can use the port.
  • The board does not answer: put StandardFirmata on it (again).
