AC voltage

Goal: see how an AC voltage changes direction, read its shape on an oscilloscope, and understand why a lamp on it gets bright twice per period.

▶ Open this example

A voltage that changes direction

A battery always gives the same voltage, with the + always on the same side: DC voltage. The wall socket gives AC voltage: the voltage rises, falls, becomes negative and rises again, over and over. The AC voltage source does that here slowed down, so that you can follow it.

  1. Start the simulation. Look at the display of the source: the voltage goes from 0 to +12 V, back to 0, to −12 V and back to 0.
  2. The oscilloscope draws that voltage over time: a sine. The highest value is the peak value, 12 V here.
  3. One complete wave takes 1 second: the period. In 4 seconds (the whole screen) you see 4 waves. The frequency is the number of waves per second: f = 1 / period = 1 Hz.

The lamp

  1. Look at the lamp: it gets bright when the voltage is +12 V, and also when it is −12 V. A lamp lights in both directions.
  2. So it gets bright twice per period, and dims briefly when the voltage passes through 0.

Assignment: another frequency

Right-click the source and choose Frequency › 2 Hz. How many waves do you see on the screen now, and how often per second does the lamp get bright?

Solution

At 2 Hz a period takes 0.5 s. In 4 seconds you see 8 waves. The lamp gets bright 2 × 2 = 4 times per second.

In real life: the mains changes direction 50 times a second (50 Hz) in Europe. An incandescent lamp then flickers 100 times a second, far too fast to see. The 230 V of the wall socket is an average (the RMS value); the peak value is about 325 V.
Why no rails here? In the previous lessons the + rail was on the left and the − rail on the right. With alternating voltage that is not possible: the two terminals of the source keep swapping roles. What is + now is − half a period later. That is why the lamp is connected straight to the source here. In the next lessons a diode turns it into a voltage with a fixed direction; from then on we use rails again.
