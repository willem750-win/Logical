The OR gate

Goal: make an output work when at least one input is on.

▶ Open this example

The buzzer has two terminals: the wire from the gate comes in on the left, and a wire goes from the right to the − rail. When the output of the gate is 1, it gives 5 V and 20 mA flows through the buzzer: enough to make it sound.

A real example: the doorbell

A house has a bell button at the front door and at the back door. Whichever button you press, the bell must ring. That is an OR gate:

Front door (O1)Back door (O2)Bell (buzzer)
000
011
101
111

Compare with the AND gate

Put the table next to the one of lesson 4. With AND everything must be on, with OR one is enough. Only when both inputs are off is the output of the OR gate off.

Exercise

Replace the OR gate in this example with an AND gate and try both buttons. When does the bell ring now? Is that still a good doorbell?

Solution

With an AND gate the bell only rings when someone at the front door and someone at the back door press at the same time. With one mouse that is not even possible. For a doorbell the OR gate is the right choice.

With a real Arduino board

Make a real doorbell with two push buttons, e.g. one at the front door and one at the back door. In the Arduino version the buttons are Arduino inputs (pins 2 and 4). The output of the OR gate makes the buzzer on the screen sound and also switches on an Arduino output (pin 8) with the real buzzer on it.

▶ Open the Arduino version

  1. Take the active buzzer from your kit: it sounds by itself when it gets 5 V. You recognise it by the sticker on top and the closed bottom. (The passive buzzer, with a green circuit board underneath, only gives a click here.)
  2. Unplug the USB cable and connect the two push buttons and the buzzer as in the diagram.
  3. Plug the cable back in, connect the board in Logic (Arduino board) and start the simulation.
  4. Press one button, then the other: each time the real buzzer sounds. That is an OR gate: one button or the other is enough.
