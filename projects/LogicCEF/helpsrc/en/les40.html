A night light with an LDR

Goal: build a light that switches on by itself when it gets dark, with an LDR, a fixed resistor and a transistor.

▶ Open this example

The LDR

An LDR is a resistor whose resistance drops as more light falls on it. With the slider below the LDR you set the light; the small screen below it shows the light, the block the resistance.

  1. Start the simulation. The LDR is at 80 % light: it has only 631 Ω.
  2. Slide to 50 %: the resistance becomes 10 kΩ. At 20 % it is already 158.5 kΩ, in the dark (0 %) 1 MΩ.

A voltage divider on the base

The 47 kΩ resistor and the LDR together form a voltage divider. The voltage on the base is the part of the 12 V across the LDR:

Ubase = 12 V × RLDR / (47 kΩ + RLDR)

  1. Set the light back to 80 %. The base gets 12 × 0.63 / 47.6 ≈ 0.16 V: less than 0.7 V. No base current flows, the transistor is off and the LED is off.
  2. Slowly slide the light lower. Around 63 % the transistor starts to conduct and the LED glows faintly; at 50 % there are already 17 mA.
  3. Below about 45 % the transistor is saturated and the LED is fully on (21 mA). It is dark, the light is on.

Where is the switching point? The transistor starts to conduct when the base gets 0.7 V. Then 11.3 V are across the 47 kΩ, so the LDR must have 0.7 / 11.3 × 47 kΩ ≈ 2.9 kΩ. That is at about 63 % light.

Task: a different switching point

Right-click the 47 kΩ resistor and choose Kind › series resistor (100 kΩ). Does it now have to be darker or less dark before the LED switches on?

Solution

Darker. With 100 kΩ the LDR must have 0.7 / 11.3 × 100 kΩ ≈ 6.2 kΩ before the base gets 0.7 V. That only happens below about 55 % light. So the fixed resistor sets at how much light the lamp switches on.

In real life: street lights, garden lights and night lights work like this. Often a potentiometer replaces the fixed resistor, so that you can adjust the switching point.
