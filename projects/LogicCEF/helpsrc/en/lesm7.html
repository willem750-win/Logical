Induction

Goal: see that a changing magnetic field in a coil produces a voltage (induction), know what its size depends on, and get to know Lenz’s law.

The other way round

In lesson 2 a current made a magnet, and in lesson 6 the Lorentz force turned a motor. Can it work the other way: can a magnet make a current? In 1831 the English physicist Michael Faraday discovered that it can, but only when the magnetic field through a coil changes. A magnet lying still in a coil does nothing.

The example

A coil of 200 turns is connected to a centre-zero meter (a galvanometer). Its zero is in the middle, so you also see the direction of the current. There is no source: the voltage has to come from the coil itself. To the left of the coil a bar magnet lies on the field line sheet.

Try it

  1. Start the simulation. Drag the magnet slowly into the coil: the needle deflects a little.
  2. Now drag it quickly into the coil: the needle deflects much further.
  3. Leave the magnet still in the coil: the needle goes back to zero.
  4. Pull the magnet out of the coil: the needle deflects to the other side.
  5. Turn the magnet round (right-click, Turn round) and try again: everything reverses.
  6. Choose 400 turns for the coil, or tick the iron core. With the core the needle deflects much further. With more turns the voltage doubles (see the hint of the coil). The current rises less, because the longer wire also has more resistance.

What is happening?

The field lines of the magnet passing through the coil are called the magnetic flux. As long as that flux changes, a small voltage arises in every turn, and all the turns together give the induced voltage. It is larger when

  • the flux changes faster (you move faster);
  • the coil has more turns;
  • the field is stronger (a stronger magnet, or an iron core that bundles the field lines).

When the flux increases (the magnet goes in), the current flows one way; when it decreases (the magnet comes out), the other way.

Lenz’s law

Lenz’s law: the induced current opposes its own cause.

Look at the picture: while the north pole of the magnet slides into the coil, the induced current turns the coil into a magnet itself, with a north pole on the side of the magnet. Like poles repel: the coil pushes the magnet back (the yellow arrow). Pull the magnet out and the coil gets a south pole there and holds it back.

So you always have to use a little force to move the magnet. That work ends up as electrical energy in the circuit. The energy does not come from nothing: it comes from your arm.

Exercise

1. When the magnet lies still in the coil, very many field lines pass through the coil. Why does the meter still show zero?

2. You want as large a current as possible. What do you do?

Solution

1. Not the field itself but the change of the field produces a voltage. If the magnet lies still, the flux does not change, and there is no induction.

2. Move the magnet as fast as possible, put an iron core in the coil, take a strong magnet and use a coil with many turns. That is why in a real bicycle dynamo or power station the magnet spins very fast: you will see that in the next lesson.
