The transistor on rails

Goal: switch a large current with a small current, and see that an electronic circuit also simply sits between the rails.

A tap that you operate with a small tap

Imagine a large tap with a small control tap next to it. If you open the small tap, that little bit of water pushes the large tap open, and a lot of water flows. A transistor does that with current:

  • the small current goes from the + rail, through the switch and the resistor, into the base (B);
  • the large current goes from the + rail, through the lamp, into the collector (C);
  • both come out together at the emitter (E) and go back to the source along the − rail.

Compare with lesson 38: it is the same circuit, but there long wires run back to the source. Here you see at a glance: the energy comes from the + rail on the left, goes through the lamp and the transistor, and back on the right via the − rail.

The voltmeter: how much voltage stays in the transistor?

The voltmeter on the right is across the transistor, between the collector (C) and the emitter (E). It measures the voltage Uce: how much of the 12 V “stays behind” in the transistor instead of in the lamp. The lamp and the transistor are one after the other (in series), so together they always get the full 12 V of the source.

SwitchTransistorVoltmeter (Uce)Lamp
opencut off: an open switch12 V0 V, off
closedsaturated: a closed switchabout 0.2 Vabout 11.8 V, on

With water: if the large tap is closed, the full pressure of the pump is on the tap, and the water wheel gets nothing. If the tap is fully open, it costs almost no pressure, and almost all the pressure goes to the water wheel. But a little resistance always stays in the tap.

That 0.2 V is the voltage drop of a saturated transistor: it is a good, but not a perfect switch. An ordinary switch or a relay contact has almost 0 V. Because of this the transistor takes a little power and gets a little warm: P = U × I = 0.2 V × 0.098 A ≈ 0.02 W. That is little, compared with 1.15 W in the lamp.

Try it

  1. Start the simulation: the switch is open, the transistor is cut off, the lamp is off. The voltmeter shows 12 V.
  2. Close the switch. Read both milliammeters and the voltmeter.
  3. Look at the electrons: in which rail are there more, and where do the two currents come together?

Exercise

How many times larger is the current through the lamp than the current in the base? Why is this useful, for example when a small light sensor has to switch on a large lamp?

Solution

About 98 mA against 1.1 mA: almost 90 times as much. A weak signal (a sensor, an output of a gate) can switch a large load this way, just as with a relay, but without moving parts. In lesson 40 an LDR does exactly that.
