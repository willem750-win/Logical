Resistors in parallel

Goal: see that each parallel branch draws its own current, that the currents add up, and work out the equivalent resistance of different resistors.

▶ Open this example

Each branch its own current

In lesson 25 you put three equal lamps in parallel. Now the resistors are different. Each branch gets the full 12 V, and Ohm's law gives the current per branch:

I = 12 V / 1000 Ω = 12 mA    I = 12 V / 470 Ω ≈ 25.5 mA    I = 12 V / 220 Ω ≈ 54.5 mA

  1. When you open the example only the top branch is on (1000 Ω). The milliammeter shows 12 mA.
  2. Switch on the second switch. The current rises to 12 + 25.5 = 37.5 mA.
  3. Switch on the third as well: 12 + 25.5 + 54.5 = 92 mA. Point at a resistor: the hint shows its own current.

The smallest resistor draws the biggest current. A branch with a small resistance is like a wide road: most of the traffic goes that way.

The equivalent resistance

Which single resistor would draw the same 92 mA? With Ohm's law: 12 V / 0.092 A ≈ 130 Ω. You can also work it out directly:

1 / Req = 1 / R1 + 1 / R2 + 1 / R3 = 1/1000 + 1/470 + 1/220 ⇒ Req ≈ 130 Ω

Note: the equivalent resistance is always smaller than the smallest resistor. Every branch you add opens an extra way for the current.

Assignment: 80 milliamps

Switch on exactly those switches that make the milliammeter show 80 mA. What is the equivalent resistance then?

Solution

Switch on the second and the third switch, the first one off: 25.5 + 54.5 = 80 mA. The equivalent resistance is 12 V / 0.080 A = 150 Ω, smaller than 220 Ω.

In real life: all the appliances in a house are in parallel on the wall sockets. The more appliances are on, the more current flows through the wiring. That is why the fuse in the fuse box can blow.
