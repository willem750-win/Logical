Amplifying with a transistor

Goal: control the base current with a potentiometer and see that the collector current is always β times larger, until the transistor is saturated.

▶ Open this example

Controlling the base current

  1. Start the simulation. The potentiometer is at 50 %: the wiper gives 6 V. Point at the transistor: the base current is Ib = (6 − 0.7) V / 47 kΩ ≈ 0.11 mA, and the collector current is 11 mA. The LED is half bright.
  2. The transistor is amplifying (orange). Check: 11 mA / 0.11 mA = 100. That is the current gain β (beta): Ic = β × Ib.
  3. Drag the wiper to the right, to 75 %. The base current drops to 0.05 mA and the collector current to 4.9 mA: again 100 times as much. The LED gets dimmer.
  4. Drag the wiper to the left, to 25 %: 0.18 mA in the base, 18 mA in the collector. The LED gets brighter.

A small change of the base current gives a 100 times larger change of the collector current. That is called amplifying.

The milliammeter in the base only shows one decimal (0.1 mA). The transistor itself shows Ib more precisely, to the right of the symbol.

The limits: off and saturated

  1. Drag the wiper all the way to the left (0 %). The base current is 0.24 mA, but the collector current stops at 21 mA instead of 24 mA. The transistor is saturated (green): the LED and the 470 Ω resistor let no more through, (12 − 2.1 − 0.2) V / 470 Ω ≈ 21 mA.
  2. Drag the wiper all the way to the right. Above about 94 % the wiper gives less than 0.7 V: no base current flows any more, the transistor is off (grey) and the LED goes out.

Between off and saturated lies the range in which the transistor amplifies. In lesson 38 we only used the two extremes: then the transistor works as a switch.

Task: a different transistor

Set the potentiometer to 50 % and right-click the transistor: Gain › β = 50. What does the collector current become? And with β = 200?

Solution

With β = 50: Ic = 50 × 0.11 mA ≈ 5.6 mA; the LED gets dimmer. With β = 200 it would be 22 mA, but the circuit only lets 21 mA through: the transistor is already saturated. Real transistors of the same type differ a lot in β. That is why you design a switch so that the base current is more than large enough.

In real life: in a music amplifier a transistor turns the weak signal of a microphone or a phone into a strong signal that can drive a loudspeaker.
