A gate drives a transistor

Goal: a logic gate, which gives only 5 V and a few milliamperes, lights a 12 V lamp through a transistor. This is where logic and power meet.

▶ Open this example

A gate only gives a little

An AND gate works on 5 V: its output gives at most 5 V, and only a few milliamperes. That is enough for a 5 V lamp or an LED, but not for a 12 V lamp that needs 100 mA. So the gate lets a transistor do the heavy work: the gate drives the base, the transistor switches the lamp.

  • The control circuit: output of the gate → 1000 Ω resistor → base B → emitter E → − rail.
  • The load circuit: + rail (12 V) → lamp → collector C → emitter E → − rail.

Try it

  1. Start the simulation. Switch on only A: the output of the AND gate stays 0, the transistor is cut off and the lamp is off.
  2. Also switch on B. The output becomes 5 V. About 5 − 0.7 = 4.3 V stands across the resistor, so about 4 mA flows into the base.
  3. The transistor is saturated and passes 98 mA: the lamp burns at almost 12 V. A small current of 4 mA from the gate switches a current 25 times larger.
  4. Switch on Show electrons at the source: you see the large current through the lamp and the transistor, and the small current from the gate into the base.

This is the two-hand control of lesson 4, but now with a real lamp: the lamp only lights when A and B are on.

Exercise: the lamp straight on the gate

Remove the transistor and the resistor and connect the lamp straight between the output of the gate and the − rail. Switch on A and B. How much voltage does the lamp get now?

▶ Open the exercise

Solution

The lamp gets only 3.5 V and barely glows (0.1 W instead of 1.2 W). The gate gives at most 5 V, and it cannot deliver much current: part of the voltage stays inside the gate itself. With a transistor the lamp gets the full 12 V of the rail, and the gate only delivers 4 mA.

In real life: a chip with gates or a microcontroller (such as an Arduino) gives at most about 20 mA per output. A lamp, a motor or a relay is therefore always driven through a transistor. Do not forget the base resistor: without it the transistor or the gate breaks.
