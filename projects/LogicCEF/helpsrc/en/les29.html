The voltage divider

Goal: see how two resistors in series share the voltage of the source between them, and work out that share yourself.

▶ Open this example

Two equal resistors

The same current flows through resistors in series. The total resistance is the sum: 470 + 470 = 940 Ω. With Ohm's law:

I = U / R = 12 V / 940 Ω ≈ 0.0128 A = 12.8 mA

  1. Switch on. The milliammeter shows 13 mA.
  2. Look at the two voltmeters: 6.0 V each. The resistors are equal, so each gets half of the 12 V.
  3. Add the two voltages: 6.0 + 6.0 = 12.0 V, exactly the voltage of the source.

Two different resistors

  1. Right-click R2 (on the right) and choose 1000 Ω. The current drops to 12 / 1470 ≈ 8.2 mA.
  2. The voltmeters now show 3.8 V across R1 and 8.2 V across R2. Together still 12 V.

The biggest resistor gets the biggest part of the voltage. You work out the part across R2 like this:

U2 = U × R2 / (R1 + R2) = 12 V × 1000 / 1470 ≈ 8.2 V

Assignment: about 4 volts

A circuit only needs 4 V, but the source gives 12 V. Choose the two resistors so that there is about 4 V across R2. You can choose from 100, 220, 470 and 1000 Ω.

Solution

4 V is a third of 12 V, so R1 must be twice as big as R2. Take R1 = 1000 Ω and R2 = 470 Ω: U2 = 12 × 470 / 1470 ≈ 3.8 V. R1 = 470 Ω and R2 = 220 Ω also gives 12 × 220 / 690 ≈ 3.8 V, but with more current (17 mA instead of 8 mA).

In real life: a volume knob is a voltage divider you can turn. Many sensors work the same way: a resistor that changes with light or heat, in series with a fixed resistor.
