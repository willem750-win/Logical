The LED and the capacitor

Goal: make an LED light safely with a series resistor, and see how a capacitor stores charge and gives it back.

▶ Open this example

Why a series resistor?

An LED is not an ordinary lamp. It only conducts above its forward voltage (a blue LED: 3.0 V), and above that the current rises very quickly. With nothing in front of it, so much current flows that it breaks at once. The series resistor takes the rest of the voltage and keeps the current small:

I = (Usource − ULED) / R = (12 V − 3.0 V) / 470 Ω ≈ 0.019 A = 19 mA

That is just below the 20 mA at which an LED gives full light. Above 30 mA it breaks.

  1. Switch on. The LED lights. Point at the LED: the hint shows the voltage and the current.
  2. Put a voltmeter across the resistor and one across the LED. Together they give (almost) the 12 V of the source.
  3. Right-click the resistor and choose a smaller value, for example 100 Ω. Work it out: (12 − 3) / 100 = 90 mA. What happens to the LED? Replace it with right-click › Replace LED and set the resistor back to 470 Ω.
  4. Turn the LED round (swap the two wires). Now it stays off: an LED conducts in one direction only.

The capacitor: charging and discharging

The capacitor is in parallel across the resistor and the LED. When the switch is on, it charges at once to almost 12 V: the blue area between the plates is full.

  1. Switch off. The LED does not go out at once but slowly gets weaker: the capacitor gives its charge back through the resistor and the LED.
  2. Watch the voltage under the capacitor. It drops to about 3 V; below that the LED no longer conducts and the rest of the charge stays.
  3. Right-click to choose a smaller capacitor (100 µF) and try again. Now the LED goes out much faster.

How long that takes is given by the time constant:

τ = R × C = 470 Ω × 0.0022 F ≈ 1 s

A bigger resistor or a bigger capacitor: longer. A smaller one: shorter.

Assignment: a lamp that stays on a little

In a car the interior light stays on for a while after you close the door. Make the LED keep glowing for about 5 seconds after switching off. Which capacitor do you choose, and which resistor?

Solution

Take the biggest capacitor, 4700 µF. With 470 Ω, τ = 470 × 0.0047 ≈ 2.2 s; with 1000 Ω, τ = 1000 × 0.0047 = 4.7 s. With 1000 Ω the LED is dimmer (I = 9 / 1000 = 9 mA), but it keeps glowing much longer.

In real life: capacitors are in almost every device. They smooth out short dips in the supply, delay a signal or, as here, let a light go out slowly.
